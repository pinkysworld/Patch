\title{Patch: State-Change Factorization and Semantic Change Contracts for Transparent Mutable Programs}
\author{Mich\'el Nguyen}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
General-purpose languages commonly expose persistent mutation through assignment or data-structure-specific updates, while auditing, undo, provenance, replay, and policy reconstruct semantic structure around those writes. Patch explores a different design: post-creation modeled persistent mutation uses a structured semantic \emph{Change} interface, and the same representation is reused to derive target-, operation-, and magnitude-aware contracts. We call the design invariant \emph{State-Change Factorization}; it is an architectural choice, not itself a compiler-correctness result.

A Lean 4 development establishes Change Signature/Capability soundness, integer range soundness, and finite exact call-tree refinement for explicit semantic fragments. Selected production direct-WebAssembly executions are additionally checked through independent transition, effect, and invocation-frame reconstruction. This reduces reliance on backend annotations while leaving capture, validator correctness, unsupported parsing, lowering, and the WebAssembly engine outside the proof.

Evaluation uses a within-artifact target-only projection to isolate the information contributed by operation and magnitude, two application cases, and a 15-site public-code mutation-shape stress test. The latter finds five application-owned single-target sites, seven additional single-target sites behind foreign persistence APIs, and three multi-target bundles. The public projects are not executed by Patch. The paper therefore claims neither real-world coverage nor superiority over richer effect, capability, or refinement systems. Its contribution is a scoped language architecture, machine-checked properties of selected fragments, and an explicit account of where the abstraction and assurance boundary stop.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} programming languages; mutation; effects; capabilities; verification; WebAssembly

\section{Introduction}
Mutable programs need information beyond the current value of a location. Debugging can require provenance, interfaces can require undo, replay requires stable transition records, and security mechanisms may need to distinguish not only which state is touched but how it changes. Conventional languages commonly reconstruct such structure around writes whose semantic intent is only partially represented at the mutation site.

Patch asks whether a small semantic mutation vocabulary can be made the ordinary interface to modeled persistent state and then reused across execution, contracts, history, and assurance. A simple update is written as:

\begin{lstlisting}
create number score = 0
change score:
  add 1
\end{lstlisting}

The same mutation vocabulary can expose authority more precise than target-only write permission:

\begin{lstlisting}
allow reward:
  score may increase up to 10

make reward(bonus number 0..5):
  change score:
    add bonus * 2
\end{lstlisting}

The declaration permits a bounded increase but not an arbitrary \texttt{set} or decrease on the same location. Richer prior systems can express equivalent or stronger state relations. Patch's question is narrower: what is gained, and what remains provable, when this restricted authority vocabulary is derived directly from the representation used for modeled mutation?

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Which semantic distinctions does the \texttt{Change} representation preserve for contract reasoning, and are those distinctions exercised by controlled mutation cases?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Which properties of the semantic-change, range, and finite-call fragments are machine checked, and how large is the formal artifact relative to the broader product surface?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] Which trust dependencies can independent transition, effect, and invocation-frame reconstruction reduce for selected production executions, and which dependencies remain?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] How does the current single-target \texttt{Change} abstraction classify selected public mutation sites, and which commit-route or grouping patterns fall outside it?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{claimboundary}
State-Change Factorization is a design invariant of the paper's semantic core. This manuscript does not claim a source-to-core compiler-correctness theorem, unique expressibility of bounded state relations, a fully verified compiler/runtime, third-party Patch execution, ecosystem prevalence, or an overhead/scalability result. The target-only comparison in RQ1 is a within-artifact ablation, not a surrogate for F*, Granule, or another prior system.
\end{claimboundary}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This manuscript makes four scoped contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item a mutation-centered language design in which post-creation modeled persistent updates use a semantic \texttt{Change} interface, together with a compact semantic core and Change Signatures/Capabilities derived from that representation;
\item machine-checked results for signature/capability soundness, a supported integer range language, and finite exact call-tree refinement, accompanied by a reproducible measurement of the handwritten and generated Lean artifact;
\item a production-assurance bridge that independently reconstructs selected transitions, semantic effects, and dynamic invocation frames before Lean checks consequences of the accepted evidence, thereby reducing specified trust dependencies without claiming verified evidence producers;
\item an evaluation that isolates operation/magnitude information through a target-only projection, exercises the distinction in two application domains, and stress-tests representational boundaries against 15 frozen public-code mutations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Language Design and Semantic Core}
\subsection{User-facing mutation discipline}
\texttt{create} introduces a fresh persistent binding. Subsequent modeled persistent mutation uses \texttt{change}; ordinary source assignment is not a second persistent-update route. The source-facing verbs are \texttt{set}, \texttt{add}, \texttt{remove}, and \texttt{clear}. Static analysis can refine numeric operations further: \texttt{add 5} is an increase, \texttt{add -5} a decrease, while \texttt{set 5} remains a set.

This discipline is intentionally narrower than Patch's complete product surface. GUI toolkit state, host APIs, I/O, and unsupported product features are not automatically turned into modeled persistent Patch state.

\subsection{Paper core syntax}
The full Patch parser contains more syntax than the mechanized fragments. For the paper, the relevant semantic core is summarized by the following grammar, where $k$ is a non-negative integer constant and $g$ denotes a supported guard:
\[
\begin{array}{rcl}
e &::=& n \mid x \mid e+e \mid e-e \mid -e \mid k\times e \\
op &::=& set~e \mid add~e \mid remove~e \mid clear \\
s &::=& skip \mid change~x\{\overline{op}\} \mid s;s \\
  &\mid& if~g~then~s~else~s \mid repeat~k~s \mid do~f(\overline e).
\end{array}
\]
Different Lean modules encode different subsets of this summary. In particular, the range theorem covers the expression grammar described in Sec.~\ref{sec:formal}, while finite-call results add ranked calls, supported guards, and static repetition. The grammar is therefore a map of the paper concepts rather than a claim that one theorem covers every production-language construct.

\subsection{Change commit rule}
Let persistent state be a map $\sigma : Name \rightarrow Value$, let $\nu(x)$ be a version counter, and let $H$ be change history. A normalized change is
\[
\delta=\langle x,b,n,v,\Delta,v'\rangle,
\]
where $\Delta$ is an ordered semantic-operation sequence, $v$ and $v'$ are before and after values, and well-formedness requires $applyOps(\Delta,v)=v'$ and $n=b+1$.

The central commit rule of the paper core is:
\[
\frac{
 v=\sigma(x) \qquad
 v'=applyOps(\Delta,v) \qquad
 \delta=\langle x,\nu(x),\nu(x)+1,v,\Delta,v'\rangle
}{
 \langle\sigma,\nu,H,change~x\{\Delta\}\rangle
 \longrightarrow
 \langle\sigma[x\mapsto v'],\nu[x\mapsto\nu(x)+1],H\cdot\delta,skip\rangle
}.
\]

We call the fact that modeled state-changing steps use this rule \emph{State-Change Factorization}, and the inclusion of the witnessing $\delta$ in $H$ \emph{Mutation Transparency}. Both are design invariants of this semantic core, because the transition system is intentionally defined this way. They should not be read as a nontrivial theorem about an independently specified source language. A stronger source-to-core result would require a separate source semantics and compiler-correctness argument, which Paper~1 does not provide.

For recipe $f$, a Change Signature $Sig(f)=\{e_1,\ldots,e_k\}$ conservatively describes target, optional field, semantic operation, and optional amount interval. A Change Capability specifies admitted authority over the same dimensions. The language-level contribution hypothesis is the reuse of this restricted semantic object, not the first invention of explicit changes, effects, capabilities, quantitative bounds, event logs, or centralized mutation.

\section{Formal Assurance and Scope}
\label{sec:formal}
The nontrivial formal claims begin after the by-construction commit discipline. They concern whether derived signatures cover modeled executions, whether magnitude analysis is sound for its expression fragment, and whether selected finite call trees refine caller signatures.

\subsection{Signature and capability soundness}
For the structured semantic core, Lean proves the inclusion
\[
RuntimeChanges(s) \subseteq Sig(s) \subseteq Cap(s)
\]
when signature inference succeeds and the inferred effects pass the executable policy check.

\begin{theorem}[Change Signature / Capability Soundness]
\label{thm:signature}
On the modeled \texttt{CoreStmt} fragment, every executed runtime change is covered by the inferred signature, and a successful policy check implies that those changes are admitted by the declared capability.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Integer magnitude reasoning}
For a supported integer expression $e$,
\[
EnvRespects(\Gamma,\rho) \wedge analyzeRange(e,\Gamma)=I \wedge eval(e,\rho)=v
\Longrightarrow v\in I.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Integer range soundness]
\label{thm:range}
If the environment respects $\Gamma$ and range analysis returns interval $I$ for a supported integer expression, every concrete evaluation of that expression lies in $I$.
\end{theorem}

The mechanized fragment includes integer literals, ranged variables, addition, subtraction, negation, and multiplication by a non-negative integer constant. Floating-point semantics, division, and general multiplication are outside this result.

\subsection{Finite exact recipe calls}
The call layer combines conservative recipe signatures with exact bindings for selected executions. Finite ranked call trees preserve nested argument bindings, supported guard choices, static repetition, and direct semantic effects. Expressions are retained for Lean re-evaluation rather than replaced by trusted precomputed constants.

\begin{theorem}[Finite transitive call-tree refinement]
\label{thm:calltree}
For a selected finite exact call tree in the encoded fragment, the accepted exact trace is covered by the caller semantic signature.
\end{theorem}

Unsupported state-dependent guards, out-of-fragment expressions, and unsupported call structures fail closed rather than being silently treated as verified.

\subsection{Quantifying the formal artifact}
The repository includes \path{scripts/report-formal-scope.js}, which counts handwritten and generated Lean files separately, reports physical/nonblank/code-line counts, and counts theorem/lemma declarations. The measurement is deliberately a size/provenance statistic, not a percentage of a vaguely defined ``whole language''. The current values are reported from the exact submission commit and can be regenerated in CI. Generated certificates are separated from handwritten proof development so certificate volume cannot inflate the apparent size of the verified core.

The broader product contains syntax, GUI/runtime paths, native packaging, host interactions, and data types outside these fragments. Consequently, the formal artifact should be understood as a set of explicitly identified semantic slices rather than formalization of the entire product language.

\section{Production Evidence and Trust Reduction}
Independent raw-source validators reconstruct supported range expressions, guards, and static call-site identity separately from the production extraction path. Selected direct-WebAssembly executions additionally expose target/before/after transitions. An independent Change-IR executor reconstructs expected transitions and semantic effects rather than accepting backend semantic annotations as authoritative.

The invocation-frame correspondence layer reconstructs dynamic caller/callee identity, invocation ordinal, parent/depth information, exact bindings, and transition boundaries without relying on backend call-entry/exit markers. For an accepted witness, Lean checks equality between reconstructed bindings and exact formal bindings, then re-evaluates frame-selected observed effects against the selected formal call tree.

The strongest regression executes \texttt{caller(1)}, \texttt{caller(4)}, and \texttt{caller(1)} through the same nested call structure. Twelve dynamic frames and six supported transitive correspondences are reconstructed; the repeated first and third roots remain distinct invocations while the middle root takes the opposite supported guard path. This addresses an attribution ambiguity that a single global effect list would leave unresolved.

\paragraph{Checked runtime correspondence.}
For accepted independently reconstructed frame evidence whose bindings equal the selected formal bindings, Lean checks that frame-selected observed effects match the exact formal call-tree statement and, on success, derives caller-signature refinement. This is a checked consequence of supplied evidence, not a theorem that the JavaScript evidence producer or runtime capture is correct.

\paragraph{Trust reduced and trust retained.}
Independent source/range, guard, call-site, transition, effect, and frame reconstruction remove specific dependencies on production annotations as the sole source of truth. They do not verify the independent validators themselves. Runtime capture completeness, validator correctness, unsupported parser/extractor behavior, JavaScript-to-WebAssembly lowering, and WebAssembly-engine correctness remain trusted.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Within-artifact information ablation}
The eight controlled cases compare full Patch Change analysis with a \emph{target-only projection} of the same reachable mutation information. The projection preserves transitively reachable target paths but deliberately erases semantic operation, magnitude, and proof-obligation information. It is an ablation study, not a competing effect/capability system. Its purpose is causal and narrow: to determine which decisions in the controlled corpus depend on the extra information Patch chooses to retain.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\paragraph{RQ1: the representation preserves decision-relevant operation and magnitude information in the controlled corpus.}
The target-only projection makes four of eight micro-case decisions less discriminating, and the application cases reproduce magnitude and direction distinctions. This supports the value of retaining these semantic dimensions inside Patch; it is not a cross-language superiority result.

\paragraph{RQ2: three nontrivial properties are machine checked for explicit fragments, whose artifact size is reported reproducibly.}
Lean establishes signature/capability soundness, integer range soundness, and finite exact call-tree refinement for the stated fragments. State-Change Factorization itself is by construction and is not counted as a theorem. The formal-scope report separates handwritten proofs/models from generated certificates and the text identifies product features outside the formal slices.

\paragraph{RQ3: independent reconstruction removes selected production annotations from the sole trusted path, but does not remove runtime or validator trust.}
Source/range, guard, static call-site, transition/effect, and invocation-frame information are reconstructed independently for supported cases before Lean checks accepted evidence. Capture completeness, validator correctness, unsupported parsing, lowering, and the WebAssembly engine remain assumptions.

\paragraph{RQ4: five of fifteen sites are direct single-target/application-owned fits; the remainder expose ownership or grouping boundaries.}
Five sites are both application-owned and single-target. Seven more are single-target but persist through foreign host APIs, and three are multi-target bundles. The study is a one-coder, JS/TS-only illustrative stress test, so these counts are not prevalence estimates.

\section{Cost and Erasure Considerations}
A mandatory semantic Change representation raises an implementation-cost question even when this paper makes no performance claim. Retaining before/after values, versions, history entries, or certificate-oriented metadata can consume time and space. Some of that information is semantically observable when history, undo, provenance, policy, or assurance consumers are active; other fields may be unnecessary in deployments that expose none of those facilities.

A plausible optimizing implementation could therefore specialize, compress, or erase non-observable Change metadata after contract checking, while preserving the state transition itself. The current artifact does not establish that such erasure is semantics-preserving, nor does it quantify the benefit. Consequently, this paper treats efficient erasure as an implementation and future-verification question rather than assuming that the research representation must be retained verbatim at runtime.

\section{Reproducibility and Artifact Boundary}
The \emph{Patch Reproducibility Bundle} binds source and generated evidence to an exact repository commit. CI regenerates formal certificates, direct-WebAssembly runtime evidence, the authority report, and application reports, then records content identities in a deterministic archive. The formal-scope script is also executed in CI.

The current product contains Patch Studio, Window tooling, native/portable deployment paths, GUI controls, and data types beyond the formal slices in this paper. These surfaces provide an implementation context but do not enlarge the formal claims. The repository also contains timing infrastructure, but hosted-CI timings are not used as performance evidence.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
The main limitations can be stated compactly. First, the paper formalizes selected semantic fragments rather than the complete source language and provides no source-to-core compiler-correctness theorem. Second, the production correspondence checks consequences of independently reconstructed evidence without verifying the JavaScript validators, runtime capture, lowering, or WebAssembly engine. Third, the information ablation identifies the contribution of operation/magnitude data only relative to Patch's own target-only projection, not relative to a named prior system. Fourth, the two application domains are internally authored and do not measure annotation burden, false rejection, or usability on a substantial external Patch codebase. Fifth, the public-code stress test contains only 15 manually classified JS/TS sites from three projects, with one coder and no inter-rater agreement measurement.

A stronger empirical follow-up could evaluate a substantial independently authored Patch program, quantify signature sizes and annotations, and measure acceptance/rejection behavior under realistic changes. A replicated multi-language mutation study could strengthen external validity. Controlled performance data and a semantics-preserving erasure argument would be required before making overhead claims. These extensions would deepen evidence but are not silently inferred from the current artifact.

Relational multi-target ChangeSets, certified host adapters, least-authority inference, temporal authority, causal debugging, and safe parallelism remain separate research questions rather than Paper~1 contributions.

\section{Conclusion}
Patch studies a language design in which modeled post-creation persistent mutation uses a semantic Change interface and the same representation supplies a restricted vocabulary for contracts, history, and assurance. State-Change Factorization is the chosen architecture, not a theorem about an independently specified compiler.

The substantive formal results are narrower and clearer: signature/capability soundness, integer range soundness, and finite exact call-tree refinement for identified fragments. Independent reconstruction further reduces selected trust dependencies when relating production direct-WebAssembly observations to those fragments, while leaving the evidence producers and runtime outside the proof.

The evaluation shows that operation and magnitude information causes four controlled decisions that disappear under a target-only projection, exercises the same distinctions in two application domains, and finds only five direct application-owned single-target fits in a deliberately small public-code stress test. Seven additional sites expose foreign commit ownership and three expose multi-target grouping. These mixed results are useful because they identify where the Paper~1 abstraction stops.

The resulting claim is deliberately architectural rather than universal: Patch couples a mandatory semantic mutation interface with automatically derived target/operation/magnitude contracts and a layered assurance path. Prior systems already establish centralized update architectures, operation-based state evolution, effects, capabilities, quantitative reasoning, provenance, and controlled mutation. Paper~1 contributes this particular constrained conjunction and evidence about its current formal and practical boundary.
